Exodus: The God Who Takes Sides

A family has become a slave population. Pharaoh turns fear into policy. The enslaved cry out — and the biblical story says God hears them.

What kind of God notices suffering from below rather than simply protecting power from above?

01 THE STORY

Genesis ends with Joseph saving Egypt from famine. Exodus opens with one sentence that undoes all of it.

“A new king arose over Egypt who did not know Joseph.”

EXODUS 1:8

Pharaoh looks at an immigrant population and sees a threat. What follows is not an outburst. It is a sequence.

Fear becomes policy.
Policy becomes forced labor.
Forced labor becomes population control.
Population control becomes the killing of children.

Pharaoh turns fear into a system.

LESSON 06 · BABEL
fear organizing civilization
LESSON 08 · EGYPT
fear organizing empire

The Israelites cry out under slavery. Exodus does not say they first become morally worthy. It says God heard.

The rescue begins because suffering is heard.

God calls Moses. Pharaoh refuses. Plagues dismantle Egypt's security. Passover marks the night of departure. The sea opens. The enslaved walk out.

Liberation comes before legislation.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

In the ancient world, kings were the guarantors of order.

A DIVINE KING

Pharaoh was understood as divine or semi-divine, personally responsible for upholding ma'at — right order in society and cosmos. To resist him was, in Egyptian terms, to side with chaos.

BRICKS AND MONUMENTS

Egyptian building ran on organized labor at enormous scale — mud brick with straw, quotas, overseers, tallies. Exodus 5 reads like an administrative dispute because that is what it is.

THE THREAT NARRATIVE

“They may join our enemies” is one of the oldest political arguments there is. Exodus records it in Pharaoh's own mouth, which is why the story keeps sounding contemporary.

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS

Semitic populations in Egypt, forced labor and vast state building projects are all well attested. The Exodus event itself is debated in date and scale — Egyptian monuments recorded victories, not departures.

Exodus tells the story from the opposite direction: what looks like order from the palace can feel like oppression from the brick pits.

Every empire tells a story about order. Exodus asks who is paying for it.

Pharaoh: when fear becomes policy

HE DOES NOT BEGIN WITH

“I hate these people.”

HE BEGINS WITH

“They are becoming too numerous.”

Fear produces a logic, and the logic sounds reasonable from inside.

There may not be enough.
They may become too powerful.
They may join our enemies.
We must control them before they threaten us.

Scarcity + fear + power = domination.

Lesson 05 introduced the suspicion that there may not be enough and that trust is dangerous. Exodus shows that consciousness scaled into government.

Which is why Pharaoh is more than one bad ruler. He becomes the Bible's standing symbol of what happens when fear and power reinforce each other — and the text pointedly never gives him a name.

God hears the cry

The rescue does not begin with Israel solving its own oppression. It begins with four verbs.

heard
remembered
saw
knew

“I have seen the affliction of my people… I know their sorrows. I have come down to deliver them.”

EXODUS 3:7–8

Salvation begins with being seen.

This becomes one of the Bible's most enduring claims about God: suffering matters to God. Not eventually, and not as a lesson — it registers, and it moves him.

03 WORDS THAT MATTER

YHWH — THE DIVINE NAME
Moses asks for a name. The answer is bound up with the Hebrew verb to be.

Ehyeh asher ehyeh has been translated several ways — I AM WHO I AM · I WILL BE WHAT I WILL BE · some mixture of both. Hebrew tense does not map cleanly onto English.

A STANDARD, NOT AN ALLIANCE · MICAH 6:8
RESCUED BEFORE THEY ARE INSTRUCTED

Israel does not receive the Ten Commandments in Egypt and then earn release by keeping them. The order is the opposite — and Exodus 20 says so in its own first line, before a single command: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out.”

THIS IS THE BRIDGE INTO LESSON 09
FREEDOM IS NOT THE SAME AS MATURITY

Leaving Egypt removes Pharaoh. It does not instantly remove Egypt from the people.

A newly freed slave population still has to learn trust, shared identity, limits, justice, responsibility — and how not to reproduce Pharaoh.

MEET US WHERE WE ARE
God enters the world people already understand.
GIVE US STRUCTURE
Covenant, law, ritual and boundaries form a people.
LEAD US BEYOND IT
External rules are meant to become internalized justice, wisdom and love.

The structure is not always the destination. Sometimes it is the container that makes the next stage possible.

LESSON 09 ASKS WHETHER TORAH CAN BE READ THIS WAY
05 THE PLAGUES

The plagues are traditionally read as acts of judgment against Pharaoh and Egypt's oppression. They also create a difficult moral problem, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

Egyptian civilians suffer.
Animals suffer.
The final plague includes the death of firstborn children.
INTERPRETATION · GENUINELY UNRESOLVED

How do we read liberation texts in which the oppressor's defeat includes catastrophic suffering?

DIVINE JUDGMENT

God decisively confronts an empire built on oppression and infanticide. Judgment on such a system is not an embarrassment; a God indifferent to it would be.

LITERARY REVERSAL

Pharaoh orders Hebrew sons killed; the story culminates in the death of Egypt's firstborn. The narrative is working measure-for-measure, which is how ancient storytelling signals justice.

THE CHRIST-CENTERED QUESTION

Christians eventually have to ask how these images relate to Jesus, who confronts enemies by a very different method — and who never gets the outcome by their destruction.

Do not erase the tension. A group that leaves comfortable here has not read the chapter.

HARDENING PHARAOH'S HEART
“Pharaoh hardened his heart.”
“God hardened Pharaoh's heart.”

Exodus says both, in different places, and never reconciles them. So the question is genuinely open: is hardening something imposed from outside — or the solidifying of a path repeatedly chosen?

Repeated refusal can become character. What we keep choosing can become what we are.

IN HEBREW IDIOM THE HEART IS WILL AND THOUGHT, NOT FEELING
◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

When oppressed people experience liberation, how easily can “God heard us” become “God hates them”?

Exodus makes a real claim: oppression matters to God. But the biblical story will have to keep developing beyond our people / their people toward justice for the vulnerable whoever they are.

Israel itself will later become capable of behaving like Egypt — and the prophets will judge Israel by exactly the standard Exodus established.

Being rescued from Pharaoh does not guarantee we will never become Pharaoh.

A RECURRING COURSE PHRASE. IT WILL COST SOMETHING WHEN WE REACH CONSTANTINE.
06 PASSOVER

Passover is not originally a Christian symbol. It is Israel's annual remembrance: we were slaves, and we were brought out.

And that memory is supposed to shape ethics. Torah keeps giving the same reason for protecting the vulnerable: remember that you were slaves in Egypt.

Memory becomes moral formation.

Christian tradition later reads Jesus' death through Passover imagery. That connection becomes richer — not poorer — if we remember what Passover already means.

Later prophets use Exodus language for the return from exile. The New Testament reaches for it around Jesus. It becomes the shape salvation has in Scripture.

Jesus and Passover

Jesus' final meal and death are narrated in the context of Passover, and early Christians interpret him through liberation and lamb imagery. The temptation is to reduce that immediately to “God needed blood.”

Passover instead gives the cross a liberation framework — which turns the question into a better one: what power are people being freed from?

Does Christ reject the Exodus concern for liberation — or deepen the way liberation is achieved?

08 THE TENSION

Does God take sides?

YES · GOD HEARS THE OPPRESSED

Exodus refuses the idea of a neutral God who is equally comfortable with slave and slaveholder.

Oppression is not morally neutral, and a God who treated it as neutral would not be good.

BUT · NOT A TRIBAL POSSESSION

The danger is turning that into God is on our side because we are us.

Later Scripture repeatedly judges Israel when Israel becomes unjust — using the same criteria.

So God's “side” may be better understood as justice, mercy and the protection of the vulnerable — not permanent allegiance to one tribe. That is a claim worth arguing about in the room rather than accepting from a page.

Think Deeper: Can you leave Egypt before Egypt leaves you?
An interpretation worth taking seriously — not a settled conclusion.

Israel leaves slavery quickly. Learning freedom takes much longer.

In the wilderness the people repeatedly long for Egypt — and remember it as a place of free food. Oppressive systems shape the imagination of the oppressed as well as the oppressor.

What happens when the structures that imprisoned us have also taught us how to think?

Freedom may therefore require more than a change of location.

new habits
new identity
new memory
new imagination
new ways of relating to power

Liberation is an event. Becoming free is a process.

WHICH IS EXACTLY WHY THE NEXT LESSON IS ABOUT A LAW
09 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

Two ancient readers who took Egypt as an image of inner bondage — and one modern one who insisted it was also still literal.

Gregory of Nyssa
c. AD 390 · THE LIFE OF MOSES

Reads Exodus as more than ancient history: Egypt becomes an image of bondage, and Moses' journey a pattern of continual movement toward God.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Liberation is not the end of growth. It is where growth becomes possible.

Gregory takes Moses' ascent as the shape of a whole life — always further in, never arriving, because God is infinite. Which fits this lesson exactly: crossing the sea is chapter one.

Augustine
c. AD 400 · HIPPO

Reads Exodus toward inner liberation — bondage to disordered desire, from which no political rescue alone can free a person.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

External freedom does not automatically create internal freedom.

Augustine knew this from his own life before he wrote it as theology. The risk in his reading is spiritualizing Egypt until Pharaoh stops being a real ruler with real bricks — which is precisely what King refused to allow.

Martin Luther King Jr.
1929–1968 · MONTGOMERY, BIRMINGHAM, MEMPHIS

Exodus became a central biblical grammar for Black Christian liberation — Egypt, wilderness, promised land — while he insisted liberation must not reproduce hatred.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The two halves of this lesson held together: God hears the cry, and the rescued must not become Pharaoh.

King belongs in a course on Christian history partly because he demonstrates something the whole course argues: a biblical story can still be forming consciousness three thousand years after it was told. Liberation theology makes the same move, with the same risk attached.
