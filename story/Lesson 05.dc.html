LESSON 05 MISTRUST · FEAR · HIDING · ALIENATION

The Fall: Where Evil Comes From

A garden, a command, a serpent, a choice — and suddenly fear enters the story. Genesis 3 may be less about God becoming angry than about humanity beginning to hide.

What if the Fall is not only about what humans did, but about how humans began to see?

01 THE STORY

Genesis 3 is only a few hundred words long, yet it has carried enormous theological weight.

A serpent questions God's trustworthiness.
The humans reach for what has not been given.
Their eyes are opened — but the first thing they see is themselves differently.
They cover. They hide. They blame.

The first visible effect of the Fall is not death. It is fear.

Before Genesis 3, the humans are described as naked and unashamed. Afterward, they experience vulnerability as danger.

They hide from one another. They hide from God. Something has changed in four relationships at once.

SELF
Shame. They cover.
OTHER
Blame. “The woman you gave me…”
CREATION
Struggle. The ground resists.
GOD
Fear. They hide.

Did God become less trustworthy — or did humanity become less able to trust?

That is one of this lesson's central questions, and the whole course keeps returning to it. Notice that nothing in the text requires us to answer it quickly.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Gardens, trees and serpents were the region's shared symbolic vocabulary.

Ancient creation and garden traditions across the ancient Near East used sacred trees, divine gardens, serpents, wisdom and immortality imagery. Genesis participates in that symbolic world while telling its own theological story.

THE SACRED GARDEN

Divine gardens — walled, watered, planted by a god — appear across Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts. In Genesis the garden is not a divine estate humans serve in as labor. It is given to them.

THE SACRED TREE

Stylized trees flanked by guardians fill Assyrian reliefs. Genesis places two trees in its garden and makes only one of them a limit — the tree of life is never prohibited.

WISDOM & IMMORTALITY

Gilgamesh hunts immortality and loses it to a snake. The region was full of stories about reaching for what belongs to the gods. Genesis keeps the shape and changes what is at stake: not luck, but trust.

THE SERPENT

Serpents carried associations with wisdom, fertility, danger and chaos, depending on the culture. Genesis is spare: it says only that this one was more subtle than any other animal God had made.

AN IMPORTANT CLARIFICATION

The serpent in Genesis 3 is not called Satan.

Later Jewish and Christian interpretation increasingly identifies the serpent with Satan or the devil. By the New Testament period that reading is widespread. But the identification is not made inside Genesis itself.

Noticing where an idea enters the tradition is not the same as denying it. It is part of reading carefully.

Notice what the humans actually reach for. The fruit is described as good for food, a delight to the eyes, and desirable for wisdom — three genuine goods. This is not a story about wanting something ugly. It is a story about taking a real good in a way that has not been given.

04 WORDS THAT MATTER

Three words that decide how the chapter gets read.

נָחָשׁ
NACHASH — SERPENT
The Hebrew word simply means serpent. Genesis itself does not call the serpent Satan.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Later Jewish and Christian readers connect the serpent with the devil, and that reading becomes standard well before the New Testament is finished. But it is an interpretive development, and Genesis is oddly restrained: the serpent is one of the animals God made.

Which leaves the more uncomfortable reading available: the voice that suggests God cannot be trusted comes from inside creation.

יָדַע
YADA — KNOW
To know — but biblical knowing is relational and experiential, not merely intellectual.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The same verb can describe intimacy, skill, and lived acquaintance. So the tree of “the knowledge of good and evil” raises questions about far more than information.

What kind of knowing are the humans trying to seize for themselves?

ἁμαρτία
HAMARTIA — SIN / MISSING THE MARK
The common New Testament word translated sin. Its broader background includes failing, going wrong, missing a mark.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

In Paul, sin is often more than isolated bad behavior. It can behave almost like a power that enslaves — something people are caught inside rather than merely choices they tick off.

That becomes important when Paul describes Adam and Christ as two different human trajectories rather than two moral records.

The serpent's first move is not violence. It is suspicion.

Can God really be trusted?

That question sits beneath much of what follows — in Genesis and in us.

And once fear becomes the lens, even God can begin to look frightening. Notice the detail the text is careful about: the humans hide before God has struck them. Their fear changes how they experience the one from whom they had previously received life as gift.

What if sin distorts not only what we do, but what we see?

A MAJOR CONSCIOUSNESS THEME FOR THE WHOLE COURSE
◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

Did God become dangerous — or did humans begin seeing God through fear?

Genesis 3 introduces a pattern that will repeat throughout religion: human fear can become projected onto God.

A frightened humanity may imagine a frightening God.
A punitive society may imagine a primarily punitive God.
A violent people may imagine divine violence as obvious.

That does not automatically mean every judgment text is projection. It gives us a question we will keep asking: what does fear make God look like?

Eventually, the Christian story will place every fearful image beside Christ.

THE HUMANS HIDE BEFORE ANY PUNISHMENT HAS ARRIVED. THE TEXT PUTS FEAR BEFORE JUDGMENT, NOT AFTER IT.
IMMATURE DOES NOT MEAN EVIL

Irenaeus imagined early humanity not as a finished adult who suddenly became defective, but more like a child created good and meant to grow.

A child can be good without being mature.

Growth requires freedom, trust, limits, failure, correction and development. That gives us another way to read Genesis 3.

Was humanity perfect and then ruined?
Or good but immature — and did it grasp for something it was not yet ready to receive rightly?

Both possibilities stay visible in this course. We develop the developmental model properly later; here it is only opened.

06 THE TENSION

Two major Christian readings. They are not simply alternatives to choose between.

Did humanity fall from perfection — or fail to grow into maturity?

FALL FROM ORIGINAL RIGHTEOUSNESS

In the Western tradition, especially under Augustine's influence, Genesis 3 becomes the foundational story of humanity falling from an original state of righteousness into sin and corruption.

Human beings inherit a damaged condition — and in many Western formulations, inherited guilt as well.

GOOD BUT IMMATURE

Irenaeus and many Eastern Christian trajectories place greater emphasis on humanity as created good but immature — made for growth into likeness with God.

The Fall becomes an early misuse of freedom inside a longer developmental journey.

These readings share something important: humanity is no longer living in the freedom and trust for which it was made.

The unresolved question is how we should describe what was lost.

perfection?
innocence?
trust?
maturity?
communion?

We are leaving the tension open. Both readings have deep roots, and a group that argues about it well has understood the lesson.

A LATER DOCTRINE TAKES SHAPE

Genesis 3 itself never uses the phrase original sin.

The doctrine develops over centuries, and Augustine becomes especially influential in the Latin West. He reads Paul — especially Romans 5 — as teaching that Adam's sin affects the entire human race in a deep inherited way.

Eastern Christianity generally emphasizes inherited corruption, mortality and alienation — ancestral sin — more than inherited legal guilt.

Much of Western Christianity's instinctive reading of Genesis 3 comes through Augustine, even when readers do not realize it. Lesson 31 takes up his theology properly.

07 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

Paul does something striking with Genesis. He places Adam and Christ side by side — not as two moral records, but as two ways of being human.

ADAM · HUMANITY TURNED INWARD
fear
grasping
disobedience
death
CHRIST · A DIFFERENT TRAJECTORY
trust
self-giving
obedience
life

Christianity therefore does not merely say Jesus fixes Adam's mistake. It says Jesus shows humanity what humanity was meant to become.

Put that beside Lesson 04. Christ is the image of the invisible God. He is also the image of restored humanity.

If Genesis 3 begins with mistrust…
Jesus' life is marked by radical trust.
If Adam grasps…
Christ gives himself away.
If fear produces hiding…
Christ lives openly before God and others.

Christ does not merely erase the Fall. Christ reveals another way of being human.

IRENAEUS CALLS IT RECAPITULATION
FREEDOM NEEDS FORMATION

If humanity is immature rather than simply defective, then limits and structure may have a developmental purpose. A child often begins with external boundaries before learning to internalize the deeper value those boundaries protect.

The command in Eden may therefore be understood not merely as an arbitrary test, but as a boundary inside a relationship of trust.

We will return to this when Israel receives Torah: external structure can become a scaffold for internal transformation.

Genesis 3 can be read as a story about disobedience. But underneath the disobedience is a prior question: can God be trusted?

The serpent does not begin by telling the humans to become violent. It begins by suggesting that God may be withholding something good. Once that suspicion enters, reality changes.

Gift→scarcity
Vulnerability→shame
Difference→blame
Limits→oppression
God→someone to hide from

What if much of what Christianity calls sin grows out of the belief that reality is not safe enough to trust?

Listen to what that belief actually says to us:

There isn't enough.
I am alone.
I must protect myself.
I must take before someone else does.
Someone must lose for me to win.

In that sense, the Fall may describe not only a broken moral record but a broken way of seeing.

If fear distorts perception, salvation may require more than forgiveness. It may require learning to see again.

08 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

Three voices — one developmental, one Western and decisive, one that keeps the whole thing inside a story of growth.

Irenaeus
c. AD 180 · LYONS

Humanity was created good but immature, and intended to grow toward likeness with God.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Created good does not mean created finished.

On this reading the Fall is an early wrong turn in a story whose destination is maturity in Christ — which is why Irenaeus can describe Christ's whole life as recapitulation, humanity's course retraced correctly rather than merely a debt discharged.

AGAINST HERESIES · THE DEVELOPMENTAL READING THIS COURSE KEEPS OPEN
Augustine
c. AD 400 · HIPPO, NORTH AFRICA

The most influential Western interpreter of the Fall: deep inherited consequences, the bondage of the will, and humanity's absolute need for grace.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Much of Western Christianity's instinctive reading of Genesis 3 comes through Augustine, even when readers do not realize it.

He is worth stating fairly rather than dismissively. His account of the will's bondage comes out of hard self-knowledge, not cruelty, and his insistence that evil is privation rather than a substance protects the goodness of creation. Whether inherited guilt follows from Romans 5 is a separate question, and the East answered it differently.

CONFESSIONS · CITY OF GOD · ON NATURE AND GRACE · TAKEN UP PROPERLY IN LESSON 31
Gregory of Nyssa
c. AD 380 · CAPPADOCIA

Sees humanity's destiny as continual movement into God, and understands salvation largely as restoration, healing and growth.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

This keeps the Fall inside a larger story of becoming, rather than making failure the final definition of humanity.

Because God is infinite, Gregory argues, the soul's movement into God never terminates. Growth is not a phase before arrival; it is the shape of the life itself.

LIVING AS THOUGH REALITY WERE OTHERWISE

Part of what Genesis 3 describes is an attempt to live as though the world were built differently than it is. Creation has been presented as gift, goodness, belonging, provision. And then a different set of assumptions arrives.

WHAT CREATION SAID
gift
goodness
belonging
provision
WHAT GENESIS 3 INTRODUCES
God may be withholding
there may not be enough
I need to grasp
I must protect myself
vulnerability is dangerous

Humans are genuinely free to live from those assumptions. The freedom is not an illusion, and the mistrust, fear and hiding that follow are real consequences of a real choice. But the freedom does not extend to making the assumptions true.

We can live against the grain of reality. We cannot make the grain disappear.

TAKEAWAY

The Fall does not begin with a world God calls evil. It begins when creatures inside a good world stop trusting the gift.

Fear enters. Shame follows. Hiding begins.

The rest of the biblical story will ask how trust is restored.

WHERE THIS GOES NEXT

Genesis 3 introduces mistrust. Genesis 4–11 shows what mistrust becomes when it spreads through families, societies, violence, revenge and empire.

What happens when fear becomes a civilization?
