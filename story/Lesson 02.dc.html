LESSON 02 c. 3000–1500 BC · MESOPOTAMIA · EGYPT · CANAAN

Before Israel: The Ancient Religious World

Long before Abraham, people were already praying, sacrificing, building temples, telling sacred stories and trying to understand the divine. Israel's story begins inside that world — not outside it.

To understand what becomes distinctive about the biblical story, we first need to understand the world it inherited.

01 THE STORY

Before Abraham, Moses, Israel or the Bible, human beings had been reaching toward the divine for thousands of years.

They built temples, prayed, offered sacrifices, told stories about the gods, watched the heavens, buried their dead with ritual, and tried to understand storms, disease, fertility, war, harvest, death and the mystery of existence itself.

For ancient people, religion was rarely a separate compartment of life. The divine world and ordinary life overlapped. Rain, drought, victory, sickness, childbirth, kingship, harvest and war could all be read through a sacred world.

Israel's story begins inside this world. Its people will use forms their neighbors would immediately recognize: sacrifice, priesthood, temples, covenant, sacred festivals, purity practices, creation stories, flood traditions and law.

That makes the biblical story more interesting, not less. The question isn't simply whether Israel shared religious forms with its neighbors. It clearly did. The more interesting question is what happens to those inherited assumptions as the story unfolds.

THE THESIS OF THIS LESSON

Israel inherits a religious world. The biblical story begins transforming it.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Israel appears surprisingly late in the ancient story. By the time Israel emerges, civilizations had already been building cities, temples, legal systems, empires and religious traditions for well over a thousand years.

MESOPOTAMIA
Sumer · Akkad · Babylon

Cities such as Ur, Uruk and Babylon developed monumental temples, priesthoods, written myths, law collections, astronomy and elaborate systems for understanding humanity's relationship with the divine. This is the cultural world the biblical story associates with Abraham's origins.

EGYPT
Kingship · temple · cosmic order

Egyptian religion connected kingship, nature, death, temple worship and cosmic order. Pharaoh was not merely a political official; Egyptian kingship existed within a sacred understanding of reality. The concept of ma'at — order, justice, balance, right arrangement — described how the cosmos and society were meant to function.

CANAAN
El · Baal · Asherah

The land in which Israel eventually develops already had its own peoples and religious traditions. Texts found at Ugarit preserve stories involving El, Baal, Asherah and a larger divine council — background that later helps explain names, images and council language inside the Hebrew Bible.

A CONNECTED WORLD
Trade · migration · empire

These cultures were not sealed off from one another. Trade, migration, conquest, diplomacy, intermarriage and empire moved stories, technologies, laws, religious symbols and ideas across borders.

Israel did not develop behind a wall. It belonged to this world.

THE CORRIDOR
SEE THIS ERA IN “EVERYTHING, HAPPENING AT ONCE” →

The biblical story develops in the corridor between some of the greatest civilizations of the ancient world — Egypt to the south-west, Mesopotamia to the north-east, and a land bridge in between that everyone's armies had to cross.

How ancient was the religious world before Israel?

A focused view. The master timeline holds the full chronology.

c. 3200–3000 BC
Writing and Sumerian cities

Cuneiform writing develops in Mesopotamia while temple-centered cities flourish.

c. 2600 BC
The great pyramids

Egypt already possesses monumental architecture, centralized government, complex ritual and developed beliefs about kingship and death.

c. 2334 BC
Sargon of Akkad

One of the earliest known territorial empires unites large parts of Mesopotamia.

c. 18th CENTURY BC
Hammurabi · Atrahasis

Babylonian law and Mesopotamian flood traditions — Atrahasis among them — provide important comparison points for later biblical material.

c. 14th CENTURY BC
Akhenaten and the Aten

Akhenaten radically elevates one god and suppresses other cults — an unusual episode in the history of Egyptian religion, undone within a generation.

By the time Israel appears in the historical record, religion is already ancient.

The gods had addresses

Ancient gods were often associated especially with particular peoples, cities, temples, territories and rulers. Babylon had Marduk. Canaan knew Baal and El. Egyptian deities could be closely tied to particular cities, temples, natural realities and royal power. A temple was understood as a meeting place between divine and human realms.

That created a religious world naturally organized around a small set of categories.

our god
our people
our land
our king
our enemies

Notice how closely the divine world can begin to resemble the human one. Human beings organize themselves into tribes, territories, kings, allies, enemies, hierarchies and wars — and the heavens can begin to look remarkably similar.

INTERPRETATION · A RECURRING THREAD OF THIS COURSE

Genesis will soon make the extraordinary claim that human beings are created in the image of God. An old line Richard Rohr often repeats turns that around:

“God made us in God's image — and we returned the favor.”

If our world is tribal, God can become tribal.
If our world is ruled by kings, God can look like a cosmic king.
If our enemies must be destroyed, we can become convinced God wants them destroyed too.
If sacrifice is assumed necessary to secure favor, we may imagine a God who needs sacrifice.

None of that proves belief in God is merely projection. It raises a harder question.

◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION

What are we discovering about God — and what are we projecting?

WATCH FOR THIS MARK. THE QUESTION RETURNS THROUGHOUT THE COURSE.

The Hebrew Bible becomes fascinating precisely because its picture of God will not stay contained by tribe, territory, temple, king, ethnicity or national interest. Israel will increasingly make a much larger claim: the God of Israel is the God of everything.

How does a tribal people arrive at a universal claim like that?

05 THE CONSCIOUSNESS SHIFT
A PARTICULAR THEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION

Much ancient religion involved learning how to live within a world populated by divine powers — how to seek favor, avert disaster, preserve order, and perform the rituals believed to connect human life with the sacred. Israel uses many of those same forms.

But within the biblical story another emphasis gradually becomes more important. God speaks. God calls. God promises. God hears. God enters covenant. Divine initiative begins moving toward the center.

FROM
Managing divine powers
THROUGH
Being addressed by God
TOWARD
Relationship and trust

Human beings still interpret God through the categories available to them. That is why this story will move unevenly. Images of God as tribal warrior, king, judge, parent, shepherd, husband, fire, wisdom, breath and eventually Father all emerge within particular worlds of human experience. Some images reveal something. Some also carry the limitations of the people using them.

The Bible preserves both astonishing breakthroughs and moments that make us wonder whether human fear, violence or tribalism is being projected onto God.

These stages are not a ladder, and they do not simply replace one another. A new vision does not instantly erase an old one. Fear, appeasement, trust, covenant, tribalism, mercy, reward, punishment and relationship will keep appearing beside one another throughout the biblical story — sometimes in the same book, occasionally on the same page.

Richard Rohr describes spiritual development as something like three steps forward and two steps backward — a useful way to watch this story. We are not tracing a smooth upward climb. We are watching genuine breakthroughs, regressions and contradictions develop inside the same tradition, which is what real human development looks like. Revelation may unfold inside that development rather than bypassing it.

What if God isn't changing? What if humanity's ability to see God is changing?

Did God invent sacrifice, or step into a practice humanity already had?

HISTORICAL OBSERVATION

Sacrifice existed long before Israel.

Human beings practiced sacrifice long before Israel existed. Food, drink, animals, valuable objects and — in some cultures and circumstances — even human beings could be offered. Sacrifice could express gratitude, allegiance, communion, purification, petition, atonement or a desire for divine favor.

THEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION WE ARE EXPLORING

God may be meeting humanity inside an already-existing sacrificial consciousness — and gradually transforming it.

When sacrifice becomes part of Israel's relationship with God, what exactly is happening? Is God introducing something God needs in order to forgive? Or is God meeting human beings inside a religious language they already understand, and gradually transforming what it means?

History tells us that sacrifice was already part of human religion long before Israel. What history cannot tell us is why sacrifice became part of Israel's relationship with God.

If people already assumed that divine powers were approached through sacrifice, then Israel entered its relationship with God with that assumption already in the air.

That raises a theological question we will follow through the rest of Scripture:

Does God require sacrifice — or does God meet people within an inherited religious system and gradually transform what sacrifice means?

06 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

Human religion often pictures humanity reaching upward — building sacred places, offering sacrifices, seeking favor, trying to cross the distance between earth and heaven. Notice the direction the biblical story increasingly takes.

God calls Abraham.
God hears slaves.
God accompanies exiles.
God speaks through prophets.
God searches for the lost.

The Word became flesh and lived among us.

JOHN 1:14

The final movement is not humanity succeeding in climbing high enough to reach God. It is God coming to meet humanity. And that gives Christianity a way of testing all our inherited images of God.

What does God look like when God is revealed in Jesus?

07 THE TENSION

A difficult question we shouldn't answer too quickly.

If Israel shares stories, laws, symbols and religious forms with its neighbors, does that make biblical revelation less unique?

TWO LENSES THAT MAY OVERLAP
DIVINE ACCOMMODATION

God communicates through forms human beings already understand. Familiar cultural forms may be the very language through which revelation becomes possible.

HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT

Israel's understanding of God develops within real history — through inherited traditions, surrounding cultures, political crises, prophetic critique, suffering, worship and new experience.

Do these have to be opposites? Could revelation be genuinely divine while also unfolding through genuinely human historical development?

And if that is possible: how do we tell the difference between divine revelation and the human assumptions through which it is being perceived?

UNRESOLVED ON PURPOSE. THIS IS ONE OF THE MAIN QUESTIONS OF THE WHOLE COURSE.
SPECULATIVE METAPHYSICAL POSSIBILITY
Think Deeper: Why is religion nearly universal?
An idea worth exploring — not necessarily something we can prove.
{{ deeperSign }}

Nearly every human society we know of developed some way of relating to what it understood as sacred, divine or transcendent. Why?

One possibility is naturalistic. Human beings are social, vulnerable, pattern-seeking creatures who know they are mortal. Religion may emerge because our minds search for agency, meaning, order, protection and explanation in an unpredictable world.

Another possibility is metaphysical. Perhaps human beings reach toward transcendence repeatedly because consciousness is actually oriented toward something real — even when that reality is perceived imperfectly.

A compass underground can still point north even when the person holding it cannot see the sky.

Archaeology cannot settle that question. Temples, prayers, sacrifices, burials, myths and sacred objects show us that humans were reaching. They cannot tell us whether anything was reaching back.

But the second possibility resonates strongly with Paul in Athens. He enters a city crowded with religious searching and does not dismiss the whole thing as meaningless. He describes people reaching for God — almost feeling around in the dark — while claiming that “he is not far from each one of us.”

There may be another layer. Ancient people used sacrifice, ritual, divination, temples and appeals to divine powers partly to navigate a world they could not control. We have science, medicine, technology, insurance, governments, savings accounts, forecasts and institutions. Yet underneath the difference remains an ancient question:

How do I make myself safe when I cannot control what happens next?

Their answers were different. The vulnerability underneath them may be surprisingly familiar. Which means religion could contain several things at once: a genuine search for transcendence, human fear, a desire for control, cultural projection, and authentic insight. These possibilities do not necessarily cancel one another.

History can show us the reaching. The deeper question is what we think the reaching means.

08 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

Can God meet humanity through incomplete understanding? Can truth appear outside the biblical tradition? Does revelation unfold pedagogically? These three were asking exactly that.

Irenaeus
c. AD 180 · LYONS

Pictured humanity as created for growth and maturity rather than instantaneous perfection — God's relationship with people as education, patient and gradual.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Against Heresies IV.38 — humanity created immature and grown toward maturity; IV.20 — God accommodating himself to human capacity. This is the earliest Christian version of the consciousness framework this course uses.

Justin Martyr
c. AD 155 · ROME

Taught logos spermatikos — the seed of the Word. People outside Christianity could encounter genuine truth, because whatever is true participates in the Logos fully revealed in Christ.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

First Apology 46 and Second Apology 13. His answer to our question: what if ancient people really were seeing something — even when they saw it imperfectly?

Clement of Alexandria
c. AD 200 · ALEXANDRIA

Could treat Greek philosophy as a tutor preparing the Greeks, somewhat as Torah prepared Israel — on the principle that all truth belongs to God wherever it is found.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Stromateis I.5 — philosophy as a schoolmaster for the Greek mind. It raises the question this lesson keeps circling: if God is revealing truth, should we expect traces of it only inside Israel, or wherever human beings genuinely seek what is real?

God meets human beings where they are. The rest of the biblical story will ask where that encounter leads.
