LESSON 01 HOW TO READ · ORIENTATION

What Kind of Book Is the Bible?

Before we ask what the Bible says, we need to ask what it is: a library formed across centuries, in many genres, through many human voices — and received by Christians as Scripture.

What does it mean to call something this human the Word of God?

01 THE STORY

When you hold a Bible, it looks like one book. That can be misleading.

The Bible is actually a library — a collection of writings produced, preserved, edited, gathered and passed down across many centuries. Its authors lived in different places, under different empires, and addressed very different circumstances.

Its pages contain law codes, family stories, royal histories, poetry, protest, wisdom sayings, prophetic speeches, letters, biographies of Jesus and strange symbolic visions. These writings do not all speak in the same way, because they were never trying to do the same thing.

Christians receive this collection as Scripture and believe that God speaks through it. But the Bible never stops being recognizably human. Its writers use the languages, literary conventions, cultural assumptions, questions and ways of seeing available to people in their own time.

That gives us one of the central questions of this entire course: how does divine revelation come to us through genuinely human voices?

ONE LIBRARY
Many writings gathered into one canon.
MANY VOICES
Poets · priests · prophets · sages · storytellers · apostles
ONE UNFOLDING QUESTION
Who is God, and what is God really like?
02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

The word "bible" comes from Greek ta biblia — "the books," plural. Chapter numbers arrive in the thirteenth century; verse numbers in the sixteenth. Nothing in the original writing was numbered.

3 LANGUAGES

Hebrew, a little Aramaic, and Greek. No book of the Bible was written in English.

~1,000 YEARS

Between the oldest and newest written material — and some of it preserves oral tradition older still.

3 CANONS

Protestant, Catholic and Orthodox Bibles do not contain the same list of Old Testament books. We will look at why in Lesson 24.

Not one kind of writing

Genre isn't just a label scholars attach to a passage. Genre helps tell us what kind of reading the passage expects from us.

Poetry can speak through metaphor. Wisdom can intentionally place competing observations beside one another. Prophets can use shocking imagery. Parables can exaggerate in order to provoke. Apocalyptic literature can turn kingdoms into beasts and political violence into cosmic warfare.

A faithful reading begins by asking: what kind of writing am I reading — and what was this kind of writing trying to do?

NARRATIVE / STORY
Genesis · Samuel · Acts

Stories that preserve and interpret the history, identity, failures and hopes of God's people. This is theological storytelling and ancient historiography — not modern journalistic reporting.

04 WORDS THAT MATTER

Important Hebrew, Greek, and historical terms that help us understand this lesson.

τὰ βιβλία
TA BIBLIA — “THE BOOKS”

Our English word Bible ultimately comes from a Greek expression meaning “the books.” The name itself contains a clue: the Bible is a collection before it is a single volume.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Early Christians spoke of the writings in the plural for centuries. Latin later took the Greek plural as a singular noun, biblia, and the singular “Bible” followed — a convenience of binding that quietly changed how readers expect the contents to sound.

תּוֹרָה
TORAH — “INSTRUCTION / TEACHING”

Often translated law, but Torah is broader than legislation. It can mean teaching, guidance, or instruction in how Israel is to live within covenant.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Calling Torah simply “law” can make it sound more like a modern legal code than the Hebrew word itself suggests — closer to what a parent gives a child than to what a state imposes on a citizen.

θεόπνευστος
THEOPNEUSTOS — “GOD-BREATHED”

An unusual Greek word, traditionally translated “inspired by God,” used in 2 Timothy 3:16.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The verse tells us something important about how early Christians regarded Scripture — but it does not by itself explain a complete theory of how inspiration and human authorship work together. That gap is where The Tension begins.

05 THE CONSCIOUSNESS SHIFT
A PARTICULAR THEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION

At an early stage of faith, certainty can feel like the same thing as faithfulness. If the Bible is God's Word, we may assume every sentence must function in exactly the same way. But Scripture itself is more complicated — and more interesting.

Proverbs can place opposite instructions beside one another. Job's friends make confident theological claims that the book eventually overturns. Ecclesiastes questions easy answers. Prophets challenge kings, priests and the nation itself. Later biblical voices sometimes reread earlier traditions from a new historical situation.

The same is true of the four Gospels. Read side by side, they do not match at every point — and the church has known this from the beginning. The differences are not hidden in obscure verses; they sit in the genealogies, the birth stories, the week of the crucifixion and the morning of the resurrection.

HISTORICAL FACT · ANYONE CAN CHECK THESE
TWO GENEALOGIES

Matthew 1 traces Jesus forward from Abraham through Solomon in three neat sets of fourteen. Luke 3 runs backward from Jesus past David's son Nathan all the way to Adam. Between David and Joseph the two lists barely agree — and they even name Joseph's father differently: Jacob in Matthew, Heli in Luke.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Ancient genealogies established identity and claim, not descent for a registry; they were routinely compressed and shaped. Matthew's fourteens (David's name in Hebrew numerals) argue that Jesus is the son of David and the true Israelite. Luke's line refuses to stop at Israel: this is a story for Adam's whole family. Older harmonizations proposed that one line runs through Mary, or through levirate adoption — possible, but the plainer reading is that each writer built the list his message needed.

THE DAY JESUS DIED

In Mark 14 the last supper is the Passover meal, which puts the crucifixion on the day of the feast. In John 19 Jesus is condemned on the day of Preparation, before the meal — dying at the hour the lambs were being killed. A full day apart.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

John's timing is not carelessness — it is his theology, stated in chronology. His Gospel opens with “Behold, the Lamb of God,” and it ends with Jesus dying as the Passover lambs die. Attempts to reconcile the two (different festival calendars in use at once) are not impossible. But notice what harmonizing costs: it flattens the very point John was making with the date.

EASTER MORNING

Who came to the tomb, whom they met and what they did next varies. Mark: three women, one young man in white, and they tell no one. Matthew: an earthquake, an angel, and the women meet Jesus. Luke: two men in dazzling clothes. John: Mary Magdalene alone, two angels, and she mistakes Jesus for the gardener.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The variation runs through the details and stops at the core: the tomb is empty, women are the first witnesses, and the risen Jesus is encountered. Historians often note that the fixed center amid loose detail is what testimony to a real event usually looks like — invented accounts tend to be tidier, and no one in that world would have chosen women as their star witnesses.

A SCENE MOVED

Jesus drives the traders from the temple in the final week in Matthew, Mark and Luke, where it helps set his arrest in motion. John 2 places the same scene at the very beginning of his ministry.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Ancient biography did not promise a strict timeline; writers arranged material to make meaning. John opens with the temple because his whole Gospel is about the true temple being Jesus' own body — the scene is his thesis statement. Two temple protests are conceivable, but John himself tells us why the placement matters.

TWO BIRTH STORIES

Matthew has magi, a star, Herod's massacre and a flight into Egypt. Luke has a census, shepherds, angels and a quiet return to Nazareth. Beyond Bethlehem, Mary, Joseph and the name, almost nothing overlaps — and Mark and John have no birth story at all.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Our Christmas pageant is a composite no Gospel actually tells. Matthew is writing Jesus as the new Moses — a murderous king, slaughtered infants, an escape to and out of Egypt. Luke is writing Jesus as good news that reaches shepherds before emperors. The nativity we picture is real to both authors and identical to neither.

THE LAST WORDS

Mark and Matthew end with “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” Luke ends with “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit.” John ends with “It is finished.” Three different final sentences.

CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Each closing line is the Gospel it belongs to, compressed: Mark's Jesus enters the depth of human abandonment, Luke's dies in trust and prays for his executioners, John's completes a work he chose. Blend all seven traditional sayings together and you get a longer list — and four portraits with their edges sanded off.

None of this was a modern discovery. Early Christians could have kept one Gospel and quietly dropped the rest; they kept four. They preferred four witnesses in tension to one flattened account — which tells us something about what they thought Scripture was for.

Scripture does not simply give us conclusions. It preserves the wrestling.

06 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

Christianity's center is not ultimately a book. It is Christ. The Bible matters to Christians because it bears witness to the story that reaches its center in Jesus.

Jesus does more than quote Scripture. He interprets it. At times he intensifies it, at times he redirects how it is applied, and repeatedly he draws attention toward mercy, justice, faithfulness and love. In Matthew 5 we hear the same pattern six times — “You have heard that it was said … but I say to you …” — and in Luke 24 the risen Jesus leads his followers back through Moses and the Prophets and teaches them to reread the story through him.

THE INTERPRETIVE CONVICTION OF THIS COURSE

We do not use the Bible to decide what Jesus is like. We allow Jesus to show us what God is like — and then learn to read the Bible through him.

Every part of Scripture deserves to be taken seriously. But for Christians, Jesus is not simply one biblical voice among many. He is the clearest revelation of the character of God.

07 THE TENSION

A difficult question we shouldn't answer too quickly.

Christians broadly agree that Scripture is both genuinely human and genuinely revelatory. Where Christians differ is in explaining exactly how those realities fit together.

Most Christian traditions are trying, in different ways, to protect both convictions: God speaks. Humans really wrote. The difficult question is what happens when we take both seriously.

TWO WAYS CHRISTIANS EXPLAIN IT
DIVINE GUIDANCE

God works through the authors so Scripture faithfully communicates what God intends to reveal. Human character in the text does not compromise divine authorship.

INCARNATIONAL / PARTICIPATORY

God's revelation comes through genuinely human voices shaped by particular languages, cultures, histories, limitations and perspectives.

If the human side of Scripture is real, then genre, history, development and even tension within the Bible may not be obstacles to revelation. They may be part of how revelation reaches us.

BOTH POSITIONS ARE HELD BY SERIOUS CHRISTIANS. WE WILL KEEP TESTING THEM.
THEOLOGICAL POSSIBILITY
Think Deeper: What if the Bible's humanity is part of the revelation?
An idea worth exploring — not necessarily something we can prove.
{{ deeperSign }}

What if God reveals truth by meeting human beings where they actually are, rather than bypassing human history, culture and consciousness?

If so, we might expect Scripture to look remarkably like the Bible we actually have: ancient, layered, diverse, sometimes unresolved, and filled with both extraordinary breakthroughs and the unmistakable fingerprints of the worlds in which its writers lived.

On this reading, revelation is not God dropping a finished answer key from heaven. It is God entering the human story and patiently drawing people toward clearer vision.

That possibility changes the question we ask when two parts of Scripture seem to pull in different directions. Instead of only asking “How can I make these passages say exactly the same thing?” we can also ask: “What is happening between these passages? Is something developing? Is someone seeing something more clearly?”

That does not automatically prove every developmental interpretation. But it opens an important possibility: the Bible's wrestling may not be a flaw in the story. The wrestling may be part of the story.

08 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

How have important Christian thinkers wrestled with the same question we're asking?

Irenaeus
c. AD 180 · LYONS

Argued that humanity is being educated and brought toward maturity within God's larger work — God teaches a growing creature at the pace it can bear. Read that way, revelation unfolds pedagogically through history rather than arriving complete.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Against Heresies IV.38 — on humanity created immature and grown toward maturity; IV.20 on God's accommodation to human capacity.

Origen
c. AD 230 · ALEXANDRIA

Believed Scripture often has depths beyond its surface or literal sense, and that difficult passages push the reader toward a deeper, Christ-centered reading — invitations rather than embarrassments.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

On First Principles IV.2–3 — the senses of Scripture, and why the text sometimes places obstacles in the literal reading on purpose.

Augustine
c. AD 400 · HIPPO

Held two ideas that matter here: Scripture contains literal and figurative forms that require discernment; and any interpretation that does not build love of God and neighbor has not yet arrived at the meaning.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

On Christian Teaching I.35–36 and III.10 — the rule of love as the test of a reading; The Literal Meaning of Genesis I.19 — the warning against insisting on readings the evidence contradicts.

TAKEAWAY

The Bible cannot be reduced to a rulebook. It is a library of people wrestling with God across history — preserving their breakthroughs, their failures, and the long process of learning to see.

Christians call it Scripture because they believe God meets us in that story — and that revelation unfolds within that encounter.
