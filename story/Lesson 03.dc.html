LESSON 03 CREATION · GIFT · THE GOODNESS OF MATTER

Genesis: Creation Is Gift

Before law, before failure, before sacrifice, and before anything humanity can earn, there is gift. What changes when the biblical story begins with generosity rather than a problem to be solved?

The first word over creation is not “fallen.” It is “good.”

Genesis 1–2

01 THE STORY

Before humanity does anything, God gives.

Light appears. Waters divide. Land emerges. Plants grow. Sun, moon and stars mark time. Living creatures fill sea, sky and land. Again and again, God looks at what exists and calls it good.

Human beings arrive late in the story.

They do not create the world.
They do not earn the world.
They do not persuade God to give them a world.

They awaken inside something already given.

Two portraits, side by side

Genesis actually gives us two creation portraits, kept next to each other on purpose.

GENESIS 1

Vast, rhythmic, almost liturgical.

Days, boundaries, repeated phrases, blessing, and rest. The camera is very far back.

GENESIS 2

Close to the ground.

Soil, breath, trees, animals, loneliness, relationship. Here God works with hands.

They tell the story differently, but both begin from the same astonishing premise: human life begins as gift.

BEFORE COMMAND → GIFT
BEFORE FAILURE → GOODNESS
BEFORE RELIGION CAN OFFER GOD ANYTHING → GOD HAS ALREADY GIVEN

That ordering matters. The biblical story does not begin with a God who needs humanity to solve a divine problem.

It begins with a God who creates, blesses and gives.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Genesis was not the ancient world's first attempt to explain creation. Israel lived among cultures that already had stories about how the world began, why human beings exist, what the gods are like, and why the world contains both order and chaos.

That means the most interesting question is not simply does Genesis resemble other ancient creation stories? Of course it does, in some ways. The more revealing question is:

What does Genesis say differently?

BROAD COMPARISON · NOT EVERY ANCIENT STORY SAYS THE SAME THING
ANCIENT MESOPOTAMIAN STORIES
GENESIS
Creation can emerge from divine conflict — as in Enuma Elish.
God creates without fighting another god.
Cosmic order follows struggle among divine powers.
Order comes through speech and separation.
Humans can be created to relieve divine beings of labor — as in Atrahasis.
Humans receive vocation, blessing and responsibility.
Kings especially may represent divine authority.
Every human — male and female — bears God's image.
Divine rest can follow the establishment of order.
Creation culminates in Sabbath — rest as blessing.

Genesis speaks the language of the ancient world — but rearranges the world that language describes.

That is exactly the pattern Lesson 02 described: God meeting humanity inside cultural forms it already possesses. Same vocabulary, different universe.

EXPLORE THIS ERA →

Who gets to bear God's image?

In the ancient world, kings could be described as representatives or images of divine authority. Statues in temples could stand for the presence of a god. Genesis makes an extraordinary move.

“God created humankind in his image… male and female he created them.”

GENESIS 1:27

The image is democratized.

Not simply the king.
Not simply priests.
Not simply the powerful.
Humanity.

To bear an image is to represent something. Humans are placed within creation not merely as consumers of it, but as creatures meant to reflect God's character and exercise responsibility within it.

If humans are supposed to represent God to creation, what kind of God are we supposed to represent?

THAT QUESTION WILL EVENTUALLY LEAD US DIRECTLY TO CHRIST.
◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

Genesis says humanity is made in God's image. Projection reverses the direction.

IMAGE
GOD → HUMANITY
PROJECTION
HUMANITY → GOD

The biblical claim is that humanity is meant to reflect God. The danger is that we continually turn the process around and make God reflect us.

Are we becoming an image of God — or making God into an image of ourselves?

A RECURRING TOOL, NOT A SETTLED VERDICT. WE WILL KEEP TURNING IT ON OURSELVES.
03 THE TEXT

“God saw everything that he had made, and indeed, it was very good.”

GENESIS 1:31

How seriously should Christian theology take God's first verdict on the material world?

GENESIS 1:26–31
The image, and the blessing

Humanity is made in God's image, male and female, and immediately blessed and given food. Responsibility arrives inside a blessing that is already spoken.

Notice the sequence within the passage itself: image, then blessing, then vocation, then food, then the verdict very good. Nothing here is offered as payment for anything.

READ GENESIS 1:26–31 · GENESIS 2:1–3
GENESIS 2:7
Dust + breath

Humans belong to the earth and receive life from God. Both halves are in the same sentence — soil and breath, not spirit trapped in matter.

This becomes useful later, when Christianity has to wrestle with ideas that treat the material body as inferior to the spiritual. Genesis 2 gives no foothold for that: the first human is earth that breathes.

04 WORDS THAT MATTER

Three Hebrew words that carry most of this lesson.

בָּרָא
BARA — CREATE
The verb translated “create” in Genesis 1:1. In the Hebrew Bible, God is characteristically its subject.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The word itself does not settle every later philosophical question about creation from nothing. Its emphasis here is on God as the source and organizer of the created world — the one who gives things their place, their boundary and their name.

טוֹב
TOV — GOOD
Good, pleasing, beautiful, beneficial, fitting, as it should be — depending on context.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Genesis repeats the word until the final verdict becomes very good. The material world is not introduced as something God reluctantly tolerates.

Goodness belongs to creation before anything goes wrong.

צֶלֶם
TSELEM — IMAGE / REPRESENTATION
An image, representation or statue — something that stands in for the presence or authority of another.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

In the ancient world an image could represent the presence or authority of a ruler or deity. Genesis applies this extraordinary language to humanity: male and female bear God's image.

Humanity's calling is therefore not merely to exist inside creation, but somehow to represent God within it.

05 THE CONSCIOUSNESS SHIFT
INTERPRETATION · A RECURRING THREAD OF THIS COURSE
FROM
Humanity exists to serve divine need
THROUGH
Humanity receives a world already given
TOWARD
Existence as gift

Some ancient creation traditions imagined humans as created partly to perform labor for the gods. Genesis gives humanity work and responsibility too — but notice the order.

Creation is already good.
Humanity is already blessed.
Food is already given.
Relationship is already offered.
Sabbath is already built into reality.
Vocation comes after gift.

Grace is not God's response after humanity fails. Grace is built into the first page.

Religion easily becomes a system for earning, appeasing, proving or securing. Genesis begins somewhere else.

Receive.

06 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

John deliberately takes us back to Genesis. The Greek Bible — the Septuagint — opens Genesis with two words, and John opens his Gospel with the same two.

GENESIS 1:1 · GREEK
ἐν ἀρχῇ
EN ARCHĒ

“In the beginning… God created the heavens and the earth.”

JOHN 1:1 · GREEK
ἐν ἀρχῇ
EN ARCHĒ

“In the beginning was the Logos…”

Then John makes an enormous claim.

“All things came into being through him.”

JOHN 1:3

Jesus is therefore not introduced as God's late solution to a creation that went wrong. The Logos belongs to the story from the beginning.

And if creation comes through the Logos while God calls material creation good, then the incarnation is not God reluctantly entering something alien or dirty. Matter was never the enemy. “The Word became flesh” intensifies a relationship between God and creation that was already there.

Lesson 04 will ask just how far the early Christians took that claim: what if Christ is not merely part of the story — but the pattern through whom the story exists at all?

07 THE TENSION

A difficult question we shouldn't answer too quickly.

If creation is good, where does everything that isn't good come from?

TWO LENSES THAT MAY OVERLAP
GOOD BUT WOUNDED

Classical Christian theology argues that evil is not a thing God created. Evil is the distortion, corruption or privation of something originally good.

Creaturely freedom allows love — but it also allows real refusal and real harm.

GOOD BUT UNFINISHED

Another Christian approach emphasizes that “good” does not necessarily mean finished, static, painless or incapable of development.

A creation capable of freedom, growth, evolution, relationship and genuine becoming may also contain risk, mortality, contingency and suffering.

Neither lens by itself resolves the deepest forms of suffering — especially disease, disaster, animal suffering, or the suffering of children. We should say that out loud rather than paper over it.

We are holding the question open.

LESSON 05 · THE FALL WILL RETURN TO IT.
THEOLOGICAL / METAPHYSICAL POSSIBILITY
Think Deeper: Is creation God's first incarnation?
An idea worth exploring — not necessarily something we can prove.
{{ deeperSign }}

Richard Rohr sometimes calls creation God's “first incarnation.” He does not mean that a tree, a planet or an atom is literally Jesus, or that creation and the incarnation of Christ are identical Christian doctrines.

He is making a larger theological claim: before God becomes visible in a human life, the invisible God is already expressing divine creativity, generosity, beauty and presence through a material universe.

Scripture itself gives Christians language for thinking this way.

“All things came into being through him.”

JOHN 1:3

“In him all things hold together.”

COLOSSIANS 1:17

If material creation exists through the Logos, incarnation does not represent God's first contact with matter. It is an extraordinary intensification of something already true: God has never been absent from creation.

This is a theological interpretation. It is not a scientific explanation of matter, and it is not the same claim as saying the universe is God — the difference between pantheism and panentheism matters here.

What changes if matter is not a barrier between God and creation — but something capable of bearing divine presence?

THAT QUESTION RETURNS AT · INCARNATION · SACRAMENTS · HEALING · RESURRECTION · NEW CREATION
08 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

Three church fathers on creation, matter and what humanity is for.

Irenaeus
c. AD 180 · LYONS

Two ideas: creation is genuinely good, and humanity was created for growth and maturation rather than as a finished, static product.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

For Irenaeus, being created does not mean being finished.

Humanity is made for development into the likeness of God. Against Heresies IV.38 makes the argument directly — and it prepares us for his very different reading of the Fall.

LEADS TO LESSON 05 · THE FALL
Athanasius
c. AD 330 · ALEXANDRIA

Argues that the same divine Word through whom creation exists is the Word who enters creation in Christ.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

On the Incarnation — creation and incarnation as one continuous act of one Logos. Which makes the incarnation restoration and fulfillment rather than a rejection of material reality.

LEADS TO LESSON 04 · LOGOS AND CREATION
Basil of Caesarea
c. AD 370 · CAPPADOCIA

His Hexaemeron — sermons on the six days of creation — treats wonder toward the natural world as a path toward contemplation of the Creator.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

For Basil, creation is not disposable scenery. It is something worth paying attention to.

He preaches about seeds, birds, tides and stars — and draws an ethic straight out of creation-as-gift, arguing that hoarding wealth is theft from the poor.

10 EXPERIENCE
GROUP EXERCISE · 25 MIN · PAPER + MARKERS
Rewrite the first page

In small groups, write a six-sentence creation story that begins:

“In the beginning, the gods were at war…”

Let them build a world from conflict.

“In the beginning, everything began as a gift…”

Then write the second story, six sentences again.

After reading both aloud, ask:

What kind of universe does each story create?
What kind of gods emerge from each?
What kind of human beings would you expect each world to produce?
How would fear, power, work, worship and relationships function differently in each story?
Then return to Genesis 1 — and say what the exercise was actually for
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The point is not simply that one ancient story is “bad” and Genesis is “good.” That comparison is too easy, and it stops the conversation.

The point is to feel how a creation story becomes a story about reality itself.
