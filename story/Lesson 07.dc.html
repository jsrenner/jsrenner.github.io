LESSON 07 PROMISE · TRUST · BLESSING

Abraham: A Promise to a Nobody

After Babel, the biblical story suddenly narrows from nations and empires to one wandering family. God's answer to a fractured world begins not with power, but with a promise.

What if being chosen was never mainly about being favored over everyone else — but being blessed for everyone else?

Election begins with purpose, not privilege.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Abraham is not a modern monotheist dropped into the ancient world.

He enters the biblical story from Mesopotamia, and Israel's own Scripture later remembers his ancestors as serving other gods.

Which means Abraham's journey is not simply geographical. It may also represent a developing understanding of who God is.

HOUSEHOLD RELIGION

Families kept their own gods — small figures, household shrines, ancestral obligations. Religion was inherited property as much as belief.

LAND AND DEITY

Gods were widely understood to belong to territories. Leaving your land could mean leaving your god's jurisdiction — which makes this call stranger than it sounds to us.

CLAN AND INHERITANCE

Identity, protection, legal standing and old age all ran through kin. A childless man outside his clan had no future and no defense.

COVENANT, SACRIFICE, HOSPITALITY

Treaties cut with animals, offerings to secure favor, obligations to strangers at the tent — the practices in these chapters were ordinary regional life.

So leaving land and kin was not merely changing addresses. In the ancient world, land, family, identity, protection, inheritance and religion were tangled into a single thing.

What does it mean to trust a God who asks you to leave the structures that previously made you feel secure?

Abraham is not a perfect hero

The Bible does not present Abraham as someone who immediately masters trust. He is called to trust — and repeatedly struggles to do it.

God promises protection…
…and Abraham lies about Sarah in Egypt.
God promises descendants…
…and they try to secure the promise through Hagar.
God promises blessing…
…and Abraham sometimes acts from fear.

Faith does not mean Abraham never doubts or controls. Faith means he keeps returning to the promise.

Which matters, because we should not turn biblical faith into psychological certainty. And it matters that the text reports the damage: Hagar pays for someone else's impatience, and Genesis gives her the only scene in the book where a person names God.

04 FAITH AS TRUST

Modern Christians often hear the word faith and think: believing the right statements. But Abraham's faith begins before creeds, doctrinal systems, or even Israel.

WHAT WE OFTEN MEAN

I am sure of the following propositions.

WHAT ABRAHAM DOES

Trust enough to move.

He does not know how the promise will happen. He does not always understand what God is doing. He does not behave with perfect consistency. But he gradually learns to entrust his future to something beyond his ability to control.

Certainty tries to eliminate vulnerability. Trust accepts it, and stays anchored anyway.

Before Abraham builds a nation. Before Torah. Before circumcision. Before Israel exists. God promises blessing.

Abraham does respond. He leaves. He trusts. He obeys. But every one of those responses happens inside a promise already given.

06 ELECTION: CHOSEN FOR WHAT?

“Chosen people” can easily be heard as God likes us more than everyone else. But Genesis 12 points in another direction, and it does so in the same sentence: Abraham is chosen so that all the families of the earth may be blessed.

CHOSEN INSTEAD OF

A boundary drawn around a favored group.

CHOSEN FOR

A vocation carried on behalf of everyone else.

Biblical election can therefore be understood as vocation. And Paul reads it exactly that way — “in you all the nations will be blessed” is, for him, the point of the whole thing.

A THREAD RUNNING THROUGH ISRAEL, THE PROPHETS, JESUS, PAUL — AND THE CHURCH'S OWN TEMPTATIONS
◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

When Abraham hears God, how much is revelation — and how much is Abraham still interpreting God through the religious world he knows?

Abraham does not leave the ancient world and instantly acquire twenty-first-century theology. He encounters God while still carrying assumptions about sacrifice, family, patriarchy, inheritance, violence and divine testing.

If revelation unfolds historically, then genuine encounter and limited understanding can coexist.

God may really speak while humans still hear through the categories available to them.

THIS IS THE FRAME WE NEED IN PLACE BEFORE GENESIS 22.
07 GENESIS 22 · ABRAHAM AND ISAAC

Genesis 22 is one of the most disturbing and influential stories in Scripture. Abraham is told to offer Isaac — the child through whom the promise is supposed to continue.

In Abraham's ancient world, the idea that gods might demand something costly was not unimaginable. Child sacrifice existed in parts of the ancient Near Eastern religious world, though its extent and specific contexts are debated.

What does Abraham think God is like at this point in the story?

WHAT THE STORY SAYS
God tests Abraham.
Abraham prepares to sacrifice Isaac.
The act is stopped.
A ram is provided.
The promise continues.
THE THEOLOGICAL QUESTION
Is God demonstrating that human sacrifice is never ultimately what God wants?
Is Abraham being led through a religious assumption he already understands, toward a different understanding of God?
Or is the story fundamentally about radical obedience and trust?

Christians have read Genesis 22 in all of these ways, and more. We are not collapsing them into one answer tonight.

△ SACRIFICE · THE QUESTION CONTINUES

We are not resolving the sacrifice question here. We are watching it develop.

REVELATION DOES NOT ERASE DEVELOPMENT

Abraham can genuinely encounter God without immediately understanding everything about God. A new revelation does not instantly erase every inherited assumption — and that turns out to be a recurring biblical pattern.

encounter → structure → partial understanding → growth → correction → deeper understanding

A new vision does not instantly erase an old one.

08 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

Blessing to the nations

Paul reads the promise to Abraham as anticipating the inclusion of the nations. The story that begins with one family eventually opens outward again.

The narrowing was always meant for widening.

GALATIANS 3:8

Trust and self-giving

Abraham is asked to trust a promise he cannot control. Jesus lives that trust completely: in Gethsemane and on the cross he asks for the outcome to change — and still does not seize it.

Christians have long heard resonances between Genesis 22 and Golgotha: a beloved son, wood carried up a hill, a father who does not withhold, provision at the last moment.

But typological resonance is a later reading looking back. It does not mean Genesis 22's original meaning was simply a prediction of Jesus. The chapter meant something to its first readers long before anyone connected it to a Roman execution.

And notice the asymmetry that makes the comparison uncomfortable: on Moriah, the son is spared.

09 THE TENSION

Is Abraham chosen because he is special — or does being chosen make him responsible?

PRIVILEGE

Election can be read as divine favor directed toward a particular people — a status held, defended, and inherited.

VOCATION

Genesis 12 immediately connects election to blessing for others — a task carried, not a rank achieved.

The biblical story will struggle with both possibilities for centuries. Israel can understand chosenness as responsibility, or as superiority — and so can the church.

Chosenness can become vocation — or tribalism.

CHRISTIANITY'S OWN RECURRING TEMPTATION IS TO TURN GRACE INTO SUPERIORITY
THEOLOGICAL / EXISTENTIAL INTERPRETATION
Think Deeper: Is faith the opposite of certainty?
An interpretation worth taking seriously — not a settled conclusion.
{{ deeperSign }}

Abraham is remembered as a person of faith. Yet his story contains uncertainty, fear, mistakes, delay, and repeated attempts to control outcomes.

PERHAPS FAITH IS NOT
I know exactly what will happen.
BUT
I trust enough to keep walking when I do not know.

Certainty often tries to eliminate vulnerability. Trust accepts vulnerability while remaining relationally anchored — which is a harder thing, and a different thing.

Much of Abraham's story asks whether reality can be trusted when the promise cannot yet be seen. That is the same question Genesis 3 put in the serpent's mouth, asked now from the other side.

Faith may not mean possessing the future. It may mean receiving the future without controlling it.

10 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

One apostle, one church father, and one nineteenth-century Dane who refuses to make Genesis 22 comfortable.

Paul
c. AD 55 · ROMANS & GALATIANS

Reads Abraham as evidence that trust and promise precede Torah — which becomes crucial to his argument about grace and the inclusion of Gentiles.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Abraham was counted righteous four hundred and thirty years before the law, and while still uncircumcised.

Paul is not being clever with dates. He is arguing from the sequence of Genesis itself: promise, then response, then sign. If Abraham belongs to God on those terms, then so can the nations.

He is included here rather than a later father because Abraham is load-bearing for his whole theology · ROMANS 4 · GALATIANS 3
Irenaeus
c. AD 180 · LYONS

Sees God's history with humanity as developmental. Abraham belongs to a long process through which humanity is gradually prepared for Christ.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

God accommodates human capacity while leading humanity toward maturity.

The same instinct we met in Lesson 05: created good does not mean created finished. Applied to revelation, it means God can meet people where they are without leaving them there — which is the frame this lesson needs for Genesis 22.

Søren Kierkegaard
1843 · COPENHAGEN · FEAR AND TREMBLING

Uses Abraham to explore the terrifying relationship between faith, ethics, trust and obedience.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

He does not resolve Genesis 22. He makes the tension sharper.

Kierkegaard circles the story from several angles and refuses every comfortable exit — including the pious one. His point is that if we find Abraham admirable without finding him frightening, we have not read the chapter. He is here to keep us honest, not to give us an answer.

WHERE THIS GOES NEXT

The promise passes from Abraham to Isaac to Jacob. The family grows. Then the family becomes enslaved.

And the next major movement in the biblical story is not humanity climbing toward God. It is God hearing the cry of oppressed people.

What does God do when the descendants of the promise become slaves?
