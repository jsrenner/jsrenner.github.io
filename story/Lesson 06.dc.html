LESSON 06 RIVALRY · VIOLENCE · REVENGE · CONTROL

Flood and Babel: The Unraveling

After Eden, the fracture spreads. A brother is murdered, revenge multiplies, violence fills the earth, and humanity builds upward in search of security and a name. Genesis 4–11 is the story of what fear becomes when it scales.

The problem is no longer one forbidden tree. It is a whole human world learning violence.

01 THE STORY

Genesis 3 ends with humans outside the garden. Genesis 4 begins with brothers.

Cain and Abel bring offerings. Cain becomes angry. God warns him that sin is crouching at the door — and that he can still rule over it.

Cain kills his brother anyway.

The first death in the Bible is not natural. It is murder.

But the story does not stop with one act of violence. Cain fears revenge, and God marks him so that no one will strike him. Then, a few generations later, Lamech sings: if Cain is avenged sevenfold, he will be avenged seventy-sevenfold.

Violence has become more than an act. It has become a culture.

By Genesis 6, the text says the earth is filled with violence. Then comes the flood. After the flood, God makes covenant not merely with Noah, but with every living creature. And after Babel, humanity is scattered rather than destroyed.

WHAT KIND OF STORY THIS IS
Genesis 4–11 is an escalation story.
AND ITS QUESTION
Will violence be answered by more violence — or interrupted?

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Genesis 4–11 uses story forms its first audience would have recognized instantly.

FLOOD TRADITIONS

Atrahasis and the Epic of Gilgamesh preserve flood traditions older than the biblical text in written form. Flood, boat, survivor, sacrifice — the shape was already known.

CITY-BUILDING

Mesopotamian literature celebrates the founding of cities as a gift of the gods. Genesis 4 attaches the first city to Cain's line — a quiet, deliberate judgment.

ZIGGURATS

Mesopotamian cities built monumental temple towers — ziggurats — meant to connect divine and human realms. Babel belongs to a real ancient cultural landscape.

GENEALOGIES & BOUNDARIES

Long genealogies and stories about the divine-human boundary being crossed are standard features of the region's literature. Genesis uses both, briskly, and never for its own sake.

So we ask the question Lesson 03 taught us to ask: what does Genesis do differently with familiar ancient story forms?

FOR THE FLOOD
Why does violence matter so much?
Why does covenant follow?
Why does the story end with a promise of restraint?
FOR BABEL
Why is the desire to “make a name for ourselves” portrayed as dangerous?
Why is scattering the response rather than destruction?

Cain, and the birth of rivalry

GENESIS 3
mistrust between humanity and God
GENESIS 4
rivalry between humans

Cain's problem is not simply that Abel exists. Cain experiences Abel's acceptance as a threat to himself.

What happens when another person's good begins to feel like my loss?

Rivalry creates a world of comparison.

His blessing means my rejection.
His success means my failure.
His existence threatens my worth.

Violence then becomes one way of trying to solve that problem — by removing the rival.

THIS OPENS A THREAD WE DEVELOP LATER · SCAPEGOATING AND THE CROSS

Lamech: when violence becomes identity

Cain commits violence and fears its consequences. Lamech boasts about violence — in what may be the oldest poem in Genesis.

CAIN
sevenfold
LAMECH
seventy-sevenfold

Violence is no longer merely something humans do. It has become something humans admire.

HOLD THIS NUMBER · WE COME BACK TO IT

Much later, Peter will ask Jesus whether forgiveness should extend seven times. Jesus answers: seventy-seven times.

What if Jesus is deliberately reversing Lamech?

The flood

“The earth was filled with violence.”

GENESIS 6:11

The flood is presented as divine judgment on a world consumed by corruption and violence. But morally the story creates one of the hardest questions in Scripture.

INTERPRETATION · GENUINELY UNRESOLVED

If violence is the problem, what do we do with a story in which God responds with catastrophic destruction?

We should not resolve that quickly, and this course will not pretend to. But notice where the narrative immediately goes.

covenant
limits
preservation
promise

And notice the reach of it. God's covenant is made not only with Noah, but with “every living creature.” That universality matters — nothing is required of the animals, and nothing is required of humanity for the promise to hold.

The rainbow becomes a sign not of humanity promising never to fail again, but of God promising restraint.

03 WORDS THAT MATTER

Three Hebrew words that carry the lesson.

חָמָס
HAMAS — VIOLENCE
Violence, wrongdoing, violent injustice. Genesis 6:11 says the earth was filled with hamas.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

This matters because Genesis identifies violence — not merely abstract rule-breaking — as the central symptom of humanity's unraveling.

The word carries a social edge: it is the violence the strong do to the weak, the kind prophets later denounce in courts and marketplaces.

בְּרִית
BERIT — COVENANT
A binding relationship established through promises and obligations. After the flood, God's covenant extends beyond one family to every living creature.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Covenant becomes one of the Bible's central ways of imagining a God who binds himself to creation and to people.

Watch how the terms change across the story. This one asks nothing in return. Sinai will.

בָּבֶל
BABEL / BABYLON
Babel is the Hebrew form of Babylon. Genesis plays on a Hebrew verb meaning to confuse.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Babylonian tradition connected the city's name with bab-ilu, “gate of god.” Genesis hears something closer to babble.

The story therefore works simultaneously as a narrative about language and as a critique of imperial human ambition. The greatest city in the world, renamed a stammer.

04 THE CONSCIOUSNESS SHIFT
FROM
Violence answers violence
THROUGH
Violence must be limited
TOWARD
Covenant and restraint

Human beings instinctively escalate. Cain kills. Lamech multiplies vengeance. Society fills with violence. Babel centralizes human power.

And Genesis repeatedly introduces boundaries.

Cain is marked — and protected from revenge.
The flood is followed by covenant.
Human violence is given limits.
Babel is scattered rather than annihilated.

A limit on violence is not yet enemy-love. But it may be a step toward it.

The Bible does not jump from Lamech directly to Jesus. It moves through stages — restraint, law, justice, mercy, forgiveness, and finally enemy-love.

And not smoothly. This is another case of three steps forward, two steps back. Human consciousness learns restraint long before it learns reconciliation — which is why “eye for eye” can be read as a ceiling on revenge rather than a demand to maximize punishment.

STAGES COEXIST · A NEW VISION DOES NOT ERASE AN OLD ONE
FIRST, STOP THE ESCALATION

A child who lashes out does not begin with a lecture on universal compassion. First, the violence has to stop. Boundaries come before internalization.

That may help us understand some of the Bible's moral development. Before love your enemies, there may need to be:

do not murder
limit retaliation
establish justice
protect the vulnerable

Restraint is not the destination. It can be the scaffolding that makes a deeper morality possible.

◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

When humans believe violence solves problems, do we imagine God solving problems the same way?

Genesis 6–9 places us inside one of the hardest versions of the question. Human civilization has become violent. The story depicts God responding with catastrophic judgment.

Is this a clear revelation of divine violence?
Is it an ancient human way of interpreting catastrophic judgment?
Or is the story preserving an ancient judgment framework while beginning to move toward covenant and restraint?

We should not answer faster than the text allows. This question stays open, and it gets harder — at conquest, at judgment, at atonement, at hell, and at the cross.

NOTICE WHAT WE ARE NOT DOING: DECIDING IN ADVANCE THAT EVERY DIFFICULT TEXT IS PROJECTION.
05 THE CHRIST CONNECTION

Genesis gives us a numerical image of escalating revenge. Lamech boasts: seventy-sevenfold. In Matthew 18, Peter asks how many times he must forgive — and Jesus answers with the same arithmetic.

GENESIS 4:24

Lamech multiplies revenge.

MATTHEW 18:22

Jesus multiplies forgiveness.

One of the clearest developmental reversals in the whole course — and Jesus does it with a number, not an argument.

And Babel meets Pentecost

Babel produces scattering and confused speech. Pentecost will later give us another multilingual scene — but Pentecost does not erase difference. People remain Parthians, Medes, Elamites, Judeans, Arabs. What changes is understanding.

Babel is not reversed by making everyone the same. Pentecost creates communion without erasing difference.

CARRIES FORWARD INTO CHURCH, INCLUSION AND UNITY
06 THE TENSION

A flood that destroys nearly everything: how is this a story moving toward mercy?

DIVINE JUDGMENT

The traditional reading sees the flood as a unique act of judgment on a world whose violence and corruption had become total.

God preserves Noah and creation, then establishes covenant. Judgment is real, and it is not the last word.

AN ANCIENT STORY BEING TRANSFORMED

Another reading emphasizes that Genesis retells a flood tradition already known across the region. The important question is therefore what Genesis changes.

Violence becomes central to the diagnosis. Humanity is preserved. Covenant follows. Divine restraint becomes part of the conclusion.

Neither reading fully removes the moral tension. Both are held by serious Christians.

The flood should remain difficult.

What if Babel is not mainly a story about architecture — but about the human desire to manufacture security, permanence, identity and control?

Fear can organize more than individuals. It can organize families, tribes, cities, economies, governments, empires — and religions.

What happens when “I must make myself safe” becomes “we must make ourselves untouchable”?

Babel may represent the opposite of trust. Humanity builds upward because it cannot trust belonging, provision, or vulnerability. Listen to what the city says:

We will secure ourselves.
We will make our own name.
We will not be scattered.

That impulse is ancient. It is also modern.

Fear does not only build walls around individuals. Fear can build civilizations.

But fear cannot finally make a safe universe.

Cain's violence solves the immediate problem of Abel. Lamech's vengeance may genuinely intimidate his enemies. Babel's centralization may genuinely produce security. Each of these can appear to work — for a while.

violence
→
more violence
revenge
→
escalation
control
→
a greater need for control

Reality eventually exposes what violence and domination promised but could never deliver. Which is not a claim that every empire falls promptly, or that suffering people brought it on themselves — history is far crueler and slower than that.

07 VOICES OF THE TRADITION

Three voices on violent texts, violent cities, and how to read both.

Origen
c. AD 240 · ALEXANDRIA & CAESAREA

Often resisted stopping at the violent literal surface of a biblical text, and looked for a spiritual reading in which the enemies destroyed are the vices within us.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

What do Christians do when a literal reading seems unlike the God revealed in Christ?

His answer — read deeper rather than discard — is one of the oldest Christian strategies for difficult texts. It has an obvious risk: allegory can be used to dissolve anything inconvenient. Origen is worth knowing precisely because both the strength and the risk are visible in him.

Augustine
c. AD 420 · HIPPO · CITY OF GOD

Understands human societies as shaped by different loves. The earthly city is marked by self-love, domination and the desire for glory.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Two cities, two loves. Ask what a society loves and you will know what it is.

He wrote it as Rome was being sacked, which is part of why it reads as it does. And it gives Babel a striking later parallel: let us make a name for ourselves. Augustine's earthly city is Babel's grandchild.

Ephrem the Syrian
c. AD 360 · NISIBIS & EDESSA

Did his theology in hymns. A voice for poetic, symbolic reading that holds mystery rather than flattening a story into one literal dimension.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

A story can carry several true meanings at once without any of them cancelling the others.

Ephrem wrote in Syriac, close to the Aramaic Jesus spoke, and taught choirs of women to sing his hymns. He is here partly to keep our range wide: not all serious Christian thought happened in Greek or Latin, and not all of it happened in prose.

TAKEAWAY

What begins as mistrust in Eden becomes violence between brothers, vengeance between families, and fear organized into civilization.

The biblical story will increasingly ask how escalation is interrupted.

Lamech multiplies revenge. Jesus will multiply forgiveness.

WHERE THIS GOES NEXT

Genesis 1–11 begins with the whole human family. By the end, humanity is scattered. Then the biblical camera suddenly narrows — from all nations, to one family.

What if God's answer to a fractured world begins with one ordinary person asked to trust a promise?
