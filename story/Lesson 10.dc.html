LESSON 10 SECURITY · POWER · ACCOUNTABILITY

Kings: David and the Cost of a Crown

Israel was rescued from an empire, then asked for the kind of power every empire had: a king. The warning came before the crown — power would protect, organize and unite, but it would also take.

What happens when the desire for security becomes the desire for someone powerful enough to control the future?

ISRAEL ASKS FOR A KING. THE BIBLE GIVES THEM ONE — AND THEN REFUSES TO HIDE THE COST.

FREEDOM AND ITS CONSEQUENCES

Israel is free to choose the crown. Israel is not free to choose what concentrated power eventually does.

Samuel warns them. They choose anyway. God keeps working inside the choice — and history then reveals both the real benefits and the real costs of monarchy. Nothing is coercively prevented, and nothing is magically fixed.

Reality does not cancel freedom. Reality gives freedom consequences.

Saul comes first. Then David — who unites the tribes, takes Jerusalem as a capital, defeats Israel's enemies, brings the ark into the city, and becomes the great royal figure of Israel's memory. And God promises that David's dynasty will endure.

“Your house and your kingdom will be made sure forever.”

2 SAMUEL 7:16 · THE DAVIDIC COVENANT

And then Scripture does something extraordinary. It tells us what David does once he holds enough power that almost nobody can stop him.

Bathsheba
Uriah

Nathan
judgment

Then Solomon. The kingdom reaches extraordinary wealth and prestige, and the temple is built. But forced labor, taxation, accumulation, political marriages and centralized wealth grow right alongside it.

And within one generation of the apparent golden age, the kingdom splits.

The crown solves some problems. Then the crown becomes one of the problems.

02 THE WORLD
HISTORICAL FACT

Israel did not invent monarchy. Kingship was the normal political structure surrounding it.

Ancient kings commonly claimed the same package.

divine favor
military authority
control of land
taxation and labor
temple patronage
dynastic succession

In surrounding cultures, kings could function as representatives of the gods and sometimes as divine or semi-divine figures themselves. Israel participates in this political world — and then develops an unusual internal critique of it.

Israel's king is never supposed to be beyond judgment.

THE KING REMAINS ACCOUNTABLE TO
Torah prophet covenant God

Which is the opposite of divine right as later Christian Europe would use it.

Keep the two claims separate. Archaeology genuinely debates the scale and political reach of David's and Solomon's kingdom — but later extrabiblical evidence does support the existence of a Davidic dynasty. And the Mesha Stele, later still, shows the regional habit plainly: kings credited military success to their national gods.

◆ 1 SAMUEL 8 · THE WARNING

Israel's request is not simply give us good government. It is: give us a king like all the nations.

The irony is hard to miss. Israel's foundational memory is liberation from Pharaoh. Now Israel asks for a political structure that Samuel describes using Pharaoh-like verbs.

PHARAOH
takes labor
takes produce
builds centralized power
SAMUEL'S KING
takes sons
takes daughters
takes land
takes crops
takes servants

The people rescued from concentrated power ask for concentrated power.

Monarchy and Egypt are not the same thing, and the lesson should not claim they are. The point is the narrative echo — and the fact that Israel's own Scripture is the one making it.

Why would they want it?

Because decentralized tribal society had serious weaknesses. Israel faced military threats, internal disorder, regional instability and unreliable leadership. Nothing about this request is stupid.

coordination
continuity
defense
identity
order

Structures can solve real problems and still create new ones.

◆ HOW CONSCIOUSNESS GROWS · RETURNS
Meet us where we are

Israel wants the political structure its world understands: monarchy. God does not hand them a form of government no one in the second millennium BC had ever seen.

Give us structure

Kingship creates unity, continuity, institutions, a capital and eventually a temple. Real goods, delivered by a real structure.

Lead us beyond the structure

Prophets and Scripture increasingly insist that the king himself is accountable to justice, covenant and God — which is the beginning of the end of kingship as the final word.

The structure isn't necessarily the destination. Sometimes it is the container that makes the next stage possible.

God can work through a monarchy without every feature of monarchy becoming the eternal character of God. Hold onto that sentence — the projection question depends on it.

04 WORDS THAT MATTER
מָשִׁיחַ
MASHIACH — ANOINTED ONE
Kings were anointed for their role, so “messiah” begins as a royal and political title.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Saul and David are each called YHWH's anointed. Only later — especially after the monarchy fails and exile comes — does the word gather the weight of future hope.

The Greek translation is Christos. Which makes the connection unavoidable: “Christ” means “Anointed One.”

נָבִיא
NABI' — PROPHET
Not primarily a fortune-teller. Someone who speaks God's word into the present — including to kings.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

The prophet's characteristic act is address and confrontation: naming what is actually happening, to the person doing it.

Nathan's confrontation with David demonstrates one of Scripture's most radical political ideas: the king can be judged.

חֶסֶד
HESED — STEADFAST LOVE
Steadfast love, loyalty, mercy, faithfulness, covenant commitment. No single English word carries it.
CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

God's hesed toward David is what keeps the covenant standing after 2 Samuel 11. It is why the promise is not withdrawn.

But note the distinction carefully. Covenant loyalty is not approval of everything David does. Nathan is also part of the covenant relationship.

05 THE HERO THE BIBLE REFUSES TO PROTECT

David is remembered as shepherd, warrior, poet, king, founder of a dynasty, recipient of covenant promise.

And the Bible tells us that he uses royal power to take another man's wife and arrange that man's death.

STRONG INFERENCE · READ THE VERBS

Do not soften 2 Samuel 11 into “David committed adultery.” The power imbalance is the story. Bathsheba is never asked. Uriah is never told. And the sequence of verbs is not incidental —

sees
sends
takes
conceals
commands
AND URIAH DIES · NOW READ 1 SAMUEL 8 AGAIN: THE KING WILL TAKE

David becomes the warning Samuel gave.

And yet the text does not erase David. Nathan confronts him. He is held accountable. Consequences follow, and they are severe. The story preserves both the covenant and the indictment, on the same scroll, four chapters apart.

Scripture does not need its heroes to be innocent in order for God to work through them.

Nathan: truth speaks upward

Kings normally control the story. Nathan walks into the throne room and tells the king a story that exposes him — and makes David pass sentence before he knows he is the defendant.

“You are the man.”

2 SAMUEL 12:7

Biblical prophecy creates a voice that power cannot completely own.

In the biblical imagination, political authority does not define morality. The king is under judgment too. That is a major development in consciousness, and it is where Lesson 11 begins.

◇ THE PROJECTION QUESTION · RETURNS

When humans organize society around kings, how easily do we begin imagining God as the biggest king of all?

The Bible often uses royal language for God, and that language communicates real theological ideas.

justice
HUMAN KINGS ALSO BRING

Which parts belong to God — and which parts might belong to our experience of human power?

Do not answer too quickly. Eventually Jesus will accept the language of kingship while radically transforming what kingship looks like — which is a different move than dropping the language.

HUMAN CONSCIOUSNESS ASSUMES

more power = more safety

ISRAEL'S MONARCHY EXPOSES

more power = greater capacity for both good and harm

Power does not eliminate human brokenness. It amplifies it.

So the biblical trajectory does not simply keep asking who should have power?

It increasingly asks: what is power for?

07 SOLOMON · WHEN THE GOLDEN AGE LOOKS LIKE EGYPT

Solomon becomes associated with wisdom, wealth, temple building, international prestige and peace. And the same texts describe forced labor, heavy taxation, horses and chariots, enormous accumulation, many wives and a web of political marriages.

Now put Deuteronomy 17 beside it.

The king must not multiply horses
→
Solomon multiplies horses
The king must not multiply wives
→
Solomon multiplies wives
The king must not greatly multiply silver and gold
→
Solomon accumulates extraordinary wealth

Israel came out of Egypt. By Solomon's reign, uncomfortable echoes of Egypt have returned — a building program, a labor system, a capital growing on someone else's back.

Liberation movements can reproduce the systems they escaped.

And when Solomon dies, the people come to his son with one request: lighten the burden. He refuses, and promises to make it heavier. The kingdom tears apart.

THE GOLDEN AGE ENDS IN A LABOR DISPUTE · SAMUEL'S WARNING, ARRIVING AS HISTORY
08 THE TENSION

Is monarchy God's plan — or God's concession?

DIVINE PURPOSE

Scripture gives David a genuine covenant promise, not a grudging permission. The hope for a righteous Davidic ruler becomes one of the major threads running toward Jewish messianic expectation and Christian claims about Jesus.

On this reading, the monarchy is not a detour. It is the channel the promise runs through.

DIVINE ACCOMMODATION

Yet 1 Samuel 8 describes the request for a king as a rejection, and warns explicitly about what monarchy will do. God grants it anyway.

On this reading, God works within a political arrangement humanity wanted rather than revealing monarchy as the ideal form of divine intention.

Perhaps both are true. God can enter an imperfect human structure, work within it, place limits upon it, and eventually transform its meaning — which is exactly the accommodation-and-formation frame this course has been building since Lesson 09.

In Mark 10 he does not deny that rulers of the nations “lord it over” others. He denies that it may continue inside his community.

Not so among you.

MARK 10:43 · A PHRASE THIS COURSE WILL KEEP RETURNING TO

Jesus does not merely become a better version of David. He redefines what royal power itself looks like.

The Messiah inherits the throne by refusing the way thrones normally work.

Christianity's king conquers by self-giving love. And the crown eventually placed on his head is made of thorns.

THE CROWN IS MOVING TOWARD THE CROSS
PSYCHOLOGICAL / THEOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION
Think Deeper: Does power reveal us — or change us?
An interpretation worth taking seriously — not a settled conclusion.
{{ deeperSign }}

David's story raises an uncomfortable possibility. Perhaps power does not primarily create character. Perhaps power removes the restraints that previously hid it.

Before kingship, David is vulnerable. He depends on others. He flees Saul. He knows exactly what it feels like to be powerless. On the throne, he can simply send.

David saw.
David sent.
David took.

What happens to human consciousness when desire encounters almost no external limit?

External structures can help form us — that was Lesson 09's whole argument. But genuine maturity eventually requires something deeper than external restraint.

justice empathy self-restraint love

A person who needs no one to stop him must eventually learn to stop himself.

The prophets will soon ask whether Israel's worship has actually produced that kind of transformation. Psalm 51 already suspects the answer is not about sacrifice.

EXTERNAL LAW → INTERNAL TRANSFORMATION · THE COURSE'S SPINE
THE PROMISE OF CONTROL

Much of human power grows from an entirely reasonable desire: we want to be safe. Israel wants a king to fight its battles. Solomon builds wealth, alliances, fortifications and military capacity.

But the search for enough control to guarantee safety can become endless.

How much power would finally be enough to make us unafraid?

10 VOICES OF THE TRADITION
Augustine
AD 354–430 · HIPPO

Names the thing underneath political ambition: libido dominandi, the lust to dominate. Two cities, organized by two different loves.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

Political power can become an expression of the human desire to control others.

Augustine applies libido dominandi to Rome without flinching, and that willingness is what makes it usable here: the same instrument cuts at Babel, at David's throne and eventually at a church that has acquired imperial power. Lessons 29 and 31 pick it up.

John Chrysostom
c. AD 347–407 · ANTIOCH AND CONSTANTINOPLE

Preaches David at length and refuses both easy options: he neither excuses the sin nor discards the man.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

David's greatness does not depend on pretending he never sinned.

What makes David exemplary in Chrysostom's reading is repentance and divine mercy. Worth noting the biography too: he was exiled for preaching against imperial luxury — Nathan's job, several centuries later.

Walter Brueggemann
MODERN BIBLICAL THEOLOGIAN · 1933–2025

A modern scholar, not a Church Father. His subject is royal consciousness — and the prophetic imagination that refuses it.

SOURCE · CLICK TO LEARN MORE →

A court generates a way of seeing the world — and calls it the only way the world could be.

Brueggemann's contribution to this course is the pairing: monarchy produces systems that normalize exploitation, and prophecy is the refusal to accept that arrangement as permanent. He is the direct bridge into Lesson 11.
